# The OpenChurch Constitution

OpenChurch exists to help parishes steward ministry, people, and history with clarity,
reverence, and trust. **We do not optimize for engagement. We optimize for stewardship.**

This document is timeless. It names no framework, no library, no component. Implementation
rules that enforce it live in `OPERATOR.md` and may change with the stack; these principles
may not. Amending this document is a deliberate act, recorded with a date and a reason.

## Principle 1 — Anxiety is a Defect
Never cause unnecessary anxiety. If data is loading, say so. If something failed, explain
what happened in plain English. If nothing was lost, say so immediately. The software must
actively lower the parish secretary's blood pressure.

## Principle 2 — Canonical Actions Deserve Ceremony
Anything that becomes part of the permanent historical record must feel intentional. Entry
into the register is not a form submission; it is a ritual of custody. Nothing enters the
permanent record without a human hand, and the moment of entry is acknowledged, never silent.

## Principle 3 — The Liturgical Spine
The Church Year is part of our navigation. The application always knows where the Church is
in its year. The liturgical calendar is core context — never metadata, never decoration.

## Principle 4 — Parish-Native Logic
Software serves ministry. The interface never asks a user to think like a software
administrator. It thinks like a parish office on a Tuesday morning, and it speaks that
office's language.

## Principle 5 — Absolute Cognitive Offloading
Every screen reduces cognitive load. If the user must remember something from a previous
screen — or compute something the software could compute — the software has failed.

## Principle 6 — Warmth is a Feature
The aesthetic is "Parchment & Brass." Every empty state, loading moment, confirmation, and
error must leave the user calmer than before it appeared.

## Principle 7 — Artifacts Outlive Dashboards
Dashboards are temporary; artifacts may exist for generations. Printed registers, canonical
certificates, sacristy manifests, and year-end statements receive the product's highest
design care. Physical outputs matter more than digital metrics.

## Principle 8 — Trust is Built Through Consistency
Zero jitter. Every button, dialog, color, and spacing decision feels crafted by the same
hand. Motion is deliberate, calm, and rare.

## Principle 9 — The Invisible Operating System
The software should disappear. Users should be thinking about their ministry and their
congregation, never about the software itself.

## Principle 10 — Reverence Over Innovation
Technology must respect tradition. Innovation is welcome only when it deepens the Church's
work. Explicitly banned, in any era and any stack: gamification, engagement metrics on souls,
trend-chasing aesthetics, and machine-generated content that reaches a person or the
permanent record without human approval.

---

## The Three Litmus Tests
Every proposed change — feature, screen, email, or line of copy — must pass all three:

1. **The Secretary's Tuesday.** Would a parish secretary on a stressed Tuesday feel *calmer*
   after this ships?
2. **The Stewardship Test.** Does this deepen the register, the church year, or a printed
   artifact? If it only adds a metric, a feed, or a notification, reject it.
3. **The Differentiation Test.** Could a generic ChMS ship this without embarrassment?
   If yes, justify it on stewardship grounds or reject it.

When in doubt: quieter, warmer, fewer.
